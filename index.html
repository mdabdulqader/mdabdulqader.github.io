The earliest signs of dementia are often buried in years of clinical notes. I work on teaching language models what to read.

Modern language models can read clinical text well, but the cost of running them grows sharply with document length — and a patient record can span years and many providers. That cost is a practical barrier to using these models in real clinics.

My research develops learned token selection: training a model to keep only the parts of a record that carry diagnostic signal, and discard the rest. The aim is dementia screening that is accurate enough to trust and cheap enough to deploy.

Research

I am supervised by Dr. Zina Ben Miled in the Phillip M. Drayer Department of Electrical and Computer Engineering at Lamar University. My current work sits at the intersection of natural language processing, efficient model inference, and clinical prediction.

Before moving into machine learning, I worked in computational materials science, using density functional theory and device-level simulation to study lead-free perovskite solar cells. Different subject, same discipline: building models, running them at scale, and judging carefully whether the results mean anything.
